ACT 1The kid who didn't accept "no" (age 10-12)

1The locked computer

Dad tried a deal first: one more year of Russian evening school, and Lior gets his own computer. Later, as punishment, Dad put a password on it. Lior learned to hack the password. Dad escalated and pulled out the power cable. Lior had a spare cable hidden.

He calls it "part of my game." It is the first time he turned a "no" into a "yes," and it's where his love of hacking and tech started.

Tone: funny, a cat-and-mouse comedy between father and son.

Visuals: small Beer Sheva apartment, chunky 2000s computer, Dad confidently walking away with the cable, kid pulling a second cable from under the bed.

2Third place in judo

At judo he came third, and his parents praised the kid who came first. Same story in basketball. That planted his engine for life: "If he can do it, I can do it too, and better." Years later he aimed that same feeling at bigger, older, funded competitors, and bought them.

Tone: quiet, meaningful. A small boy on the podium, looking up at step 1.

3The contrarian at the religious school

At the religious "Russians' school" in Beer Sheva he refused everything: the kippah, the assigned seat, music lessons. His inner voice: "Don't have it? I'll have it. He's better? No, I'm better."

Tone: cheeky, rebellious kid energy.

4The skateboard hustle, age 12

He wanted a skateboard. Nobody was buying it for him. So he fixed the neighbors' computers and earned his first money, around 100 shekels. At the same age he built a school forum that became the main way students at his school talked to each other.

Visuals: a 12-year-old knocking on neighbors' doors with a screwdriver, then cruising down the street on a new skateboard.

ACT 2The teenage online hustler (age 14)

5The $30 phone that started everything

At 14 he wanted to buy himself a smartphone cheaply. Browsing a small Chinese website, he noticed the exact same phone, with the exact same photos, selling for more on eBay. He listed it on eBay, sold it within about 3 hours, ordered it from China, and made around $30 profit. He didn't know the method had a name. It was dropshipping, and it became his whole career.

Visuals: a teenager's bedroom at night, two browser tabs side by side, the eBay "Item sold!" notification lighting up his face.

6The e-cigarette business and getting kicked out of school

He started selling e-cigarettes to classmates. Payment upfront, products shipped from China straight to his parents' apartment, about 30 days delivery. The apartment became a warehouse. It turned into an online store and his service became popular all over Israel.

He got kicked out of school at 14. He stopped the e-cig business after the second expulsion threat. His parents, in his words, "gave up."

Tone: funny and a bit wild. Boxes stacked in the living room, Mom stepping over packages, the principal's office.

Hook line that's approved: "At 14, I got kicked out of school. 9 years later, I became a millionaire."

ACT 3The soldier and the 100 stores (age 18-22)

7The army test he demanded

In the army he was placed in an IT technician team. He refused to just accept it, taught himself the course material, demanded a test, passed, moved up and became a team leader. His words: "Nobody came to me and said prove yourself. I said it."

Do not name his army unit.

8eBay shuts him down, he opens 100 stores

At 21, eBay suspended his main account. It was 7 years old and it was his entire income. Most people would quit. He opened 100 new stores instead.

Running 100 stores by hand was chaos: prices changing, products going out of stock. So he wrote software for himself, a "black screen" that ran all 100 stores automatically, found trending products on Amazon and listed them on eBay.

Visuals: a wall of 100 browser windows, then one black terminal screen calmly running everything.

9The black screen at the conference

At an e-commerce conference in Tel Aviv, everyone on stage showed strategies. Lior showed his black screen. Sellers lined up to ask for it. He saved their numbers in his phone as "eBay dropshipping 1," "eBay dropshipping 2" and so on. Two months later they were his beta users. That software became AutoDS.

There was no website and no billing system. Users got an invoice at the end of the month and paid for the month that had already passed.

Tone: underdog moment. Funny detail: the phone contact list full of "eBay dropshipping 1, 2, 3..."

He founded AutoDS on his own in early 2017, at 22, while working as a freelance developer to pay the bills. He's clear he never tells people to quit their jobs. He did the math first.

ACT 4Building AutoDS

10THE $1 BUG - the 3 AM story

Early days. Lior was doing everything alone: code, customer support, marketing, the accountant, legal. He ran a test that was supposed to set ONE product to $1. Instead, the software repriced every product in the system to $1. Tens of thousands of products that normally sold for $40-50.

Around 3 AM, which he calls "a normal working hour for me," a customer messaged him. She woke up to a flood of sale notifications. He replied: "Congratulations, what do you want from me?" She answered: they all sold for $1. More than 30 orders. His reaction: "Sh*t, I'm in trouble."

The danger was real: on eBay, cancelling a lot of orders gets a seller banned. Her whole business was on the line.

What he did:

  • Stayed up with her and talked her through it personally
  • Helped her write honest messages to her buyers so eBay wouldn't block her
  • Took full blame, never said "it's eBay's fault"
  • The next day went live on Facebook to ALL AutoDS customers, apologized and explained exactly what happened

The ending: nobody left. A competitor offered 20% off to any AutoDS customer who switched. Nobody moved.

And the customer? She was an Israeli who had moved to Finland and couldn't find work there. Lior felt he had wrecked her business, so he hired her. She started at $200 a month, then $400, growing with the company. She became Head of Customer Support with around 100 people under her, and stayed 6+ years.

Tone: starts as comedy at 3 AM, turns into panic, ends as a heartwarming arc.

Visuals: dark room, glowing phone, a screen full of "$1.00" price tags, a nervous Facebook Live, then years later a confident woman leading a big support team.

11The VAT crisis and fear of the stage

In year one he put every dollar into growth and didn't know about the year-end VAT bill. An advisor wanted $30K he didn't have. He took a loan, and together with the advisor built a mentorship program: 150 people paying $200 a month. That paid off the debt.

To sell it, he had to organize his first conference and speak on stage. He's an introvert. He says it was "horrible for me in the beginning." He did it anyway, and today he speaks on big international stages.

12Choosing a co-founder for fun, not for money

His first partner left early. For about a year and a half he did legal, marketing, dev and accounting alone. When he looked for a CTO, one option was a famous CTO 30 years older who would have guaranteed fundraising. He chose his friend Michael instead: "I decided that I want to enjoy the journey."

Then Ofir joined for "some SEO questions." Lior told him: "I don't have work for you... okay, come join."

Fun coincidence: Lior, Michael and Ofir are all from Beer Sheva, knew each other from the army, and their parents live close to each other.

How the three decide: they try together. If they can't, Ofir decides marketing, Michael decides tech, and if it's a three-way fight, "I'm deciding because I'm the CEO."

13Laptops from home

AutoDS was fully remote years before COVID. There was no budget for computers, so early hires brought their own laptops from home. The company worked out of WeWork for 9 years before moving to its own HQ in early 2026.

14100 videos in 100 days

Before hiring anyone for content, Lior recorded 100 educational videos himself, one a day for 100 days. AutoDS now has 1,000+ free videos.

15The giant he feared, then bought

The market leader was run by two brothers: business-school grads, older, funded, connected. Everyone said AutoDS couldn't beat them. Lior overtook them in 5 years. Then he offered to buy them. They said no. A year later, "maybe." After about 2.5 years, and a few beers, they said yes. The founder's reason for waiting: "he didn't have trust in me." Lior earned that trust over 2.5 years.

AutoDS went on to acquire DSM-Tool, ViralVault, Salefreaks and Yaballe.

16COVID: hiring while everyone fired

In 2020 competitors froze and fired staff. Lior hired a head of HR and asked her to double the company. He calls it "the best decision I've ever done." Within a year, AutoDS led the market.

175 years of an empty bank account

For the first 4-5 years everything went back into the company. "The bank was on zero always. And if it wasn't on zero, I was not satisfied." People kept asking why he didn't take money home.

18Build Your Store

In 2023, at Shopify's conference, Shopify shared that a big chunk of new stores never finish setting up. Lior saw the same thing inside AutoDS. He used AI to remove that barrier and launched Build Your Store: pick a niche, get a fully designed store with 10+ trending products, free for the user. 1M+ stores created. A year later AutoDS became Shopify's #1 partner, two years in a row.

ACT 5The big "no" and the exit

19Walking away from $38M, the day before signing

Two years before Fiverr, a private equity firm approached him. 5-6 months of negotiation, full due diligence. The offer grew from $20M to about $38M. Life-changing money.

But every question was about who to fire and which services to cut. Their plan: "Optimize it, and then milk it." Many of his employees had been with him since day one.

The final straw: he asked for MORE money if he beat their ambitious revenue targets, and they refused. To him that proved they didn't want growth. One day before signing, he walked away.

Every night during those months he came home and told his girlfriend, today his wife Galit: "We are going to exit soon." Then: "No, I don't want this one." She backed him the whole way.

This is his record-breaking story online. Approved headline: "Turned down $38M. Then sold to Fiverr for $92M."

20The bankers who quit on bid day

His first investment bank ran a big process: Boston, 24 meetings back to back. On the day bids were due, an email arrived: the whole 4-person banking team had moved to another bank. Momentum gone. His lesson: "People buy founders, people buy people."

Second try: a banker he already trusted. Lior made him promise he wouldn't leave. 50-60 presentations, several offers, some higher than Fiverr.

21Fiverr: whiskey at 8 PM, 23 days

Fiverr won on trust, not price. Late-night whiskey at their office nearby, 8-9 PM. "They're not buying the company to milk it." The deal closed in 23 days, after the 6-month PE grind. $92M. Lior was 28. AutoDS was the first e-commerce automation company bought by a public company, and Lior stays CEO.

22Forbes 30 Under 30

After the exit, Lior made the Forbes Israel 30 Under 30 list, at 29. The kid who got kicked out of school at 14, with no degree and no connections, on the Forbes list.

Visuals: the Forbes page, then a match cut back to the 14-year-old leaving the principal's office.

23Dad's reaction: "Okay."

His father always believed a good life means a real job. Lior never shared much about AutoDS with him. On exit day he called so his dad would hear it from him first. His father said: "Okay."

Tone: deadpan comedy with heart. Hold on Dad's face. Keep Dad dignified, never the butt of the joke.

24"Can your CTO still find a job?"

Until the exit, his parents kept asking when he'd get a degree and why he isn't a developer like his friends. With thousands of customers, his mom asked if he could still become a programmer. Recently she asked whether his co-founder, the CTO, "can still find a job as a programmer." They still ask him to stop posting about the exit.

Tone: pure comedy. Mom at the kitchen table, totally sincere.

25The exit watch

After the exit he nearly walked out of the store without a Patek Philippe, even though it holds its value. Galit stopped him: "It's your exit watch." The root is his mom, who still says "Money can disappear." His therapist gave him homework: walk into Lululemon and buy whatever he'd been thinking about. "It was a process."

Tone: funny, relatable, a little vulnerable.

  • She is the one who said "It's your exit watch."

His team closed one of the year's best deals while he was offline. Years earlier, his laptop came to every wedding and vacation.

ACT 7Lior today

  • Life is a game. He says it constantly. There's no finish line, only the next level. After the exit: "Now I have it, but I still want to keep playing the exact same game."
  • Works harder than ever after the exit: "That's my game." AutoDS now makes more than $50M a year, with fewer employees than at the acquisition, thanks to AI.
  • The Saturday ritual: every Saturday he opens next week's calendar and cuts meetings from 60 to 30 to 15 minutes. Personal development is the one thing never cut, about 10 hours a week. Two coaches and a therapist.
  • Energy check-ins: his assistant asks him daily: mood 1-10, did you eat, did you train. The data showed evenings were his low point.
  • The hotel ritual: after a 3-week trip wrecked his gym progress, the first thing he does in any hotel is order protein and fruit to the room.
  • The AI brain: he records every meeting and voice note so his AI knows him. "My Claude will be different than your Claude."
  • Tennis, one rule: he's not trying to be the best in the world. "You are not supposed to win everywhere."
  • The 10K: signed up for a 10K run even though he hates running. Just another challenge.
  • Wellness after the exit: ice baths, meditation, WHOOP, travel.
  • 3 employees quit to start their own companies, and he's proud of it.
  • Kids one day: no pressure, "obviously entrepreneurship," he jokes. Just be the best at whatever you pick.
