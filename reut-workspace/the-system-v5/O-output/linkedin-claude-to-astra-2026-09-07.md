## Why this clears the no-tool-tips rule

The tutorial exception needs three things at once: insider intel rather than teaching, an operator
confession to open, and a strategic close. Reut's spine hands us the confession for free, the
payload is three judgement calls rather than an install route, and the close is about running two
suppliers rather than about a product. That is the 93K post's shape, without its install steps.

**"vs." :** the tool you trust vs. the tool that checks it.

## Draft

--- POST STARTS ---

Claude has been my second brain for months.

This week something outperformed it.

People have been telling me to try the other one since spring. I ignored every one of them, because everything I work with lives inside the setup I built there. My rules, my standards, the way I brief it.

That is the real reason nobody switches. Not loyalty. You spend months teaching a tool how you work, and moving means going back to generic output on day one.

That cost collapsed this week. The import now finds what is already on your machine and carries it across. Your rules, your skills, your recent history. Nothing is deleted and the old one keeps running.

Which turns the interesting question into a management question, and there the answer is not the one everybody is shouting.

Three calls, if you are about to do this.

Move your rules first, then make the new tool read them back to you in plain English. Most of us cannot recite what we wrote eight months ago, and you are about to hand it to something new.

Run both for two weeks on the same real task. Not a demo. Something where you already know the right answer, so you are testing your brief and not the marketing.

Do not migrate the trust. A tool that has been right for a year gets checked less. A new one inherits that and has earned none of it. That gap is where the first bad thing ships.

Then the part I did not expect.

You can have one of them review the other's work. Someone ran that this week. The review caught the real fault, and invented three problems that were not real. The second model argued all three back out.

Neither of them would have caught that alone.

So no, Claude is not dead. It has a colleague now, and the two of them disagree with each other, which is the most useful thing that happened to my week.

How long would it take you to leave the tool you use most?

# v2 — rebuilt on the squint-test rhythm (Reut, 2026-09-07)

Reut's note: the block rhythm is the format. Short, medium, long, varied, never uniform.
Squint until the words blur; if every block is the same size, rewrite. Source is a LinkedIn
creator post she sent, "I only hit Post when it's beautiful."

Also her correction, and she was right: v1 turned Hills' concrete steps into vague judgement
calls to dodge the tool-tip rule, which removed the only useful part. The 93K tutorial exception
allows real steps when the framing is insider intel plus operator confession plus strategic close.
v2 uses the real steps.

Hook used: option 1 of the seven offered.

--- POST STARTS ---

Claude has been my second brain for nine months.

I moved out in four minutes.

The switch was supposed to cost a month of teaching a new tool how you work, your rules, your standards, the way you brief it. That is the only reason anybody stays.

That stopped being true this week.

Here is the whole thing.

1. Import it.

Settings, then Import, then Claude Code. It scans your machine, shows you what it found, and copies your rules, your skills and your last month of chats. Nothing gets deleted, and Claude carries on exactly as it was.

2. Let it rename your rules.

CLAUDE.md becomes AGENTS.md on the way in. Same file, same rules, new name.

3. Chase what it left behind.

The import moves your files. It does not move your shortcuts, your subagents or your hooks. Those are the parts you built last, and the parts you will miss first.

Four minutes.

Then the part nobody mentions.

Do not migrate the trust. A tool that has been right for a year gets checked less, and a new one inherits that without earning any of it. That gap is where the first bad thing ships.

So both are running here now.

One reviews the other's work, they disagree in front of me, and last week that argument caught a problem neither would have found alone.

Claude is not dead.

It has a colleague.
