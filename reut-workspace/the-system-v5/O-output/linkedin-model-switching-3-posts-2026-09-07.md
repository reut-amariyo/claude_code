# POST 1 — the switch that stays cheap

Donor: Donnelly's phased roadmap. Time-phased is the strongest save shape on LinkedIn because the
reader cannot act on it in one sitting.

Framed as vendor switching with the model as the live example, not as a migration how-to. That is
what keeps it out of the tool-tip lane. Strip the word "model" and the post still stands.

--- POST STARTS ---

Moving our agent stack to a new engine took one evening.

Getting to where that was possible took nine months.

Everyone is arguing about which model won this week. That argument only matters if you are stuck with the answer.

Here is how a switch gets cheap, in the order it actually happens.

Before you pick anything

Write down what a vendor would need to know to do the work. The company, the rules, the voice, the standard you accept. Plain text, in a folder you own. That folder is the asset. The vendor is not.

First week

Give the same brief to the old one and the new one. Not a demo task. A real one where you already know the right answer. You are testing your brief more than you are testing them.

First month

Find every place your setup only works with one supplier. Say each one out loud. Every one is a payment you have agreed to make later without noticing.

Ongoing

Nothing important lives inside a chat history. The day that rule slips is the day leaving gets expensive again.

We changed engines in June because the pricing changed. It took an evening, and it will take an evening next time.

Every model wins for about six weeks. The only durable question is whether you can leave.

What in your stack could you not walk away from tomorrow?

# POST 2 — what does not survive the switch

Donor: our own org-chart shape. Anti-checklist, one of the five legal save-first formats, and the
one nobody else is writing this week. Everybody is publishing what the new model can do. Nobody is
publishing what you lose on the way in.

--- POST STARTS ---

Nine months of instructions moved across in four minutes.

Five things did not.

The files are the easy part, and the tools now move those for you. What follows is what stays behind, in the order it hurts.

1. The parts you built last.

Your shortcuts, your small automations, the fixes layered on top. Built last, break first, and nobody remembers writing them.

2. The reason behind every rule.

The rule file moves. The argument that produced each line does not. Six months on, somebody deletes a rule because it looks arbitrary, and it was not.

3. Everything the old setup did quietly.

You never wrote it down because it always happened on its own. You find these one at a time, in production.

4. Your team's muscle memory.

The instructions are portable. The habit of how people brief the thing is not, and for a week the output gets worse for reasons nobody can name.

5. The trust.

People stop checking a tool that has been right for a year. A new one inherits that trust without ever earning it. That is where the first bad thing ships.

None of these are reasons to stay. We switched anyway.

They are the reason a four-minute migration takes a month, and the reason to plan for a month.

Which of the five would hit you hardest?

# POST 3 — the three numbers ⚠️ needs your override

Donor: Ruben Hassid's verdict post, the one you sent. His shape is flat verdict, three numbered
criteria, reasoning, the not-paid-by-anyone credibility beat, close.

What is kept: the shape. What is dropped: his verdict itself, the newsletter funnel, the ↳ arrows,
the "I might be the reason you switched" line, and any claim that one vendor beat another. Lior
does not do vendor verdicts, he does decision rules, and the swap makes it a save object instead
of a hot take with a shelf life of one week.

--- POST STARTS ---

Three numbers decide every vendor switch we make.

Which model is smarter is not one of them.

1. What the switch costs.

Not the price on the page. The hours, the week of worse output, the things that only worked in the old one. Nearly always bigger than the invoice.

2. What staying costs.

Twelve months of the gap, not this month's. A gap you can live with today compounds into a team working slower than the one across the street.

3. How fast we could leave again.

This is the one people skip, and it decides the other two. If the answer is never, the first two numbers do not matter, because you are not making a decision, you are picking a place to be stuck.

Nobody pays us to prefer a supplier. We changed engines in June because the third number was good, and we will change again the day it stops being.

The market is loud this week about which model won. It will be loud about a different one in six weeks.

Run the three numbers instead.

Which of the three do you actually calculate before you sign?
