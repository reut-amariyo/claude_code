## 3. Why It Failed

### Primary Suspect: The Image (Graph)

Owner flagged the graph image as a likely failure cause. This aligns with established patterns:

* **Graph images signal "report" not "person."** LinkedIn algorithm favors personal photos, selfies, and behind-the-scenes moments. A graph screams corporate content / newsletter promo — users scroll past.
* **No face = no connection.** Posts 15 (4K) had personal context. A faceless graph removes the human element entirely.
* **Graph contradicts the voice.** Lior's voice is conversational, punchy, personal. A data visualization signals academic/corporate tone before the reader even reads a word.

### Secondary Factors

**1. Zero personal stake.**
No "my company" story. No "I tracked tokens and found X." This is pure commentary — the same take any AI influencer could write. Fails the Lior Test: "Would someone know this is Lior without seeing the name?"

**2. Unverified claim as hook.**
"Companies are now checking productivity by tracking AI tokens" — which companies? No source, no proof. Reads as speculation dressed as fact. Loses credibility with senior audience (CEOs, founders).

**3. Repetitive structure creates fatigue.**
"Read that again." + "Not X. Not Y. Z." + "One person... One team..." — these are LinkedIn cliche patterns. Lior's audience has seen this format thousands of times. It signals "content creator template" not "founder insight."

**4. No friction or debate.**
Everyone in AI agrees tokens matter. No controversial angle. Nothing to argue with = nothing to comment on = no algorithm boost.

**5. Generic insight, no specificity.**
"One person with AI does the work of three" — no proof, no story, no numbers from Lior's companies. Compare to Post 15 where "240+ employees" and "Fiverr exit" gave concrete weight.
