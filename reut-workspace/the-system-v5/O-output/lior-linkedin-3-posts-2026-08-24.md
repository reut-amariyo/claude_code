## POST 1 — save-first · agentic commerce · structure A

--- POST STARTS ---
AI shoppers convert 42% better than humans.

Most sellers are still measuring for the old one.

We count visitors. Sessions. Time on page. Every one of those assumes a person is browsing.

An agent does not browse. It compares, decides, and leaves. One pass, no second visit, no
abandoned cart email that ever gets opened.

So the unit changes. Stop counting visits. Start counting comparisons you survived.

Five things an agent checks before it buys, and most catalogues fail at least two:

1. Whether your product data is machine readable, not only human readable
2. Whether your price resolves without a click
3. Whether stock is real time or a cached guess
4. Whether shipping cost appears before checkout
5. Whether your returns policy exists as text a machine can parse

That is catalogue work. It is the cheapest thing on your roadmap and the least glamorous.

The sellers who win the next two years will not be the ones with the best looking storefront.
They will be the ones whose data survives being read by something that does not care how the
page looks.

What is your catalogue missing?
--- POST ENDS ---

circled in red by hand. Personal-artifact family.
**Length:** ~1,180 chars.

## POST 2 — save-first · AI-native org design · structure B

--- POST STARTS ---
I gave an agent access to real customer orders.

Then came the rules that should have been written first.

Everyone is racing to put agents into production. Almost nobody publishes the guardrails,
because guardrails are boring and demos are not.

Five questions we now ask before any agent touches a live order:

Can one person reverse it in under a minute?
Does a human see it before the customer does?
What happens when it is unsure, does it stop or does it guess?
Who gets paged when it is wrong at 3am?
Would you explain the decision to the customer out loud?

That last one kills more ideas than the other four combined.

Running 250 people teaches you that the expensive failures are never the dramatic ones. They
are the quiet ones nobody owns.

An agent with no owner is a liability with excellent uptime.

Which of the five would your team fail today?
--- POST ENDS ---

**Visual:** photo of the actual list, whiteboard or a real internal doc. Never a designed card.
**Length:** ~1,050 chars.
**⚠️ Confirm before publishing:** the five questions are operator-plausible but written by me.
Lior should replace any that do not match how the team actually works. A checklist he did not
write is the one thing that will read as false.

## POST 3 — personal · structure C

--- POST STARTS ---
Go on camera when it breaks.

(Everything says hide.)

We shipped a bug that got the price wrong. Real orders, real money, real people who trusted
us with both.

The instinct in the room was a careful written note. Legal-safe. Passive voice. The kind of
message that explains without ever admitting.

I went on Facebook Live and said what happened.

Worst hour of that quarter. Best thing we did all year.

Here is the trade nobody tells you about. A written apology buys you silence. A face buys you
the benefit of the doubt the next time something breaks, and in this business there is always
a next time.

Most founders spend a crisis protecting the brand. The asset was the willingness to be seen
owning it.

You cannot build that reputation during the crisis. You can only spend it.

What did you do the last time something broke on your watch?
--- POST ENDS ---

**Visual:** a still from the actual Facebook Live, or the original notification that surfaced
the bug. Personal-artifact family.
**Length:** ~980 chars.
