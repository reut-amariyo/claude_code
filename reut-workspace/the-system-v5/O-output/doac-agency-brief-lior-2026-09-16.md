# Lior Pozin, talking points for The Diary of a CEO agency call
Prepared 2026-09-16 for the AutoDS team member speaking with the DOAC agency on 2026-09-17.
Everything below is sourced from Lior's canonical bio, two podcast appearances on record, and verified public facts.

---

## One-line intro

Lior Pozin, 31. Built AutoDS from his parents' apartment in Beer Sheva at 21 with zero investors, bootstrapped it to 200 employees and $20M+ ARR, and sold it to Fiverr at 28 in a deal that closed in 23 days. Still the CEO, still running it independently.

---

## 6 achievements, in the order that lands

1. Bootstrapped to exit. AutoDS was profitable from day one, never raised a dollar, and was acquired by Fiverr, a public company, for a reported $92 million. First e-commerce automation software ever bought by a public company.

2. 23 days. The Fiverr deal went from first conversation to signed in 23 days. Two years earlier he had walked away from a private equity buyout one day before closing, after 6 months of due diligence.

3. Scale. 1.8M+ e-commerce sellers use AutoDS, they have earned $1B+ through it, 800M+ products in the catalog, 8 marketplace integrations, Trustpilot 4.7 from 1,700+ reviews.

4. Acquirer, not only acquired. As CEO he bought 4 companies, including DSM Tool, the market leader he was afraid of when he started and overtook within 5 years.

5. Shopify's #1 partner two years in a row, 2024 and 2025. Forbes 30 Under 30 nominee at 29. Forbes Business Council member.

6. Serial builder now. Co-founder and CEO of BuildYourStore.ai, CreateUGC.AI and ShopShark alongside AutoDS. Investor.

---

## The story in 5 beats

1. Immigrant kid. Parents came from Ukraine to Israel. Mom ran a grocery store, dad fixed air conditioners. Grew up in Beer Sheva.

2. Age 10. Dad locked the family computer with a password as punishment. Lior taught himself to hack it. Dad pulled the power cable. Lior kept a spare. That is the first "no" he refused.

3. Age 12 to 14. Fixed neighbours' computers to buy a skateboard. First eBay flip at 14, $30 profit on a phone from China. Ran an online store from his bedroom with stock in his parents' apartment. Expelled from one high school in 10th grade.

4. Age 21. eBay banned the account he had built for 7 years, his entire income. Instead of quitting he opened 100 accounts, drowned in the chaos, and wrote software to run them. Other sellers asked for it. That software became AutoDS. He did not know fundraising existed, which is why the company was bootstrapped.

5. Age 28. Sold to Fiverr. Parents were still asking when he would finish his degree. Today they ask him to stop posting about the exit.

## 9 conversations Lior can carry for a full hour

1. The $1 bug. Early AutoDS, he was coding alone at 3 AM and a test repriced every product in every customer store to $1. Hundreds sold in hours. A customer woke up to a flood of sales notifications, he replied "congratulations", then realised what he had done. He talked her through saving her eBay account, then went live on Facebook to every customer the next day and took full blame. Nobody left. A competitor offered 20% to anyone who switched. Nobody moved. He hired the customer whose business he had wrecked, at $200 a month. She went on to run a 100-person support team.

2. Saying no to $30 million a day before closing. The PE buyer planned to cut staff and milk the company. Many employees had been with him since day one on below-market salaries. He walked. Two years later Fiverr paid a reported $92M. The lesson he tells: know exactly what you are not willing to sell.

3. Bootstrapped by ignorance, then by choice. He did not know raising money was an option. By the time he did, the company was profitable and he had no time for decks. His view on the AI era: SaaS needs creativity, not capital. Deep tech and hardware need money, software no longer does.

4. Running out of money while growing. Every dollar went back into growth, and at year end he discovered a VAT bill he could not pay. Took a $30K loan for an advisor, built a mentorship program with 150 people paying $200 a month, doubled revenue in a month and saved the company. Learned to sell and to speak in public in the same crisis.

5. Fear of the market leader. DSM Tool had two brothers, MBAs, connections and money. He had a laptop. He tracked them for 5 years, passed them, and bought them. Childhood engine behind it: judo, third place, his parents praised the kid who came first. "If he can, so can I."

6. "I live on 200." He sold the company and did not slow down. 10 hours a week on personal development, 2 coaches and a therapist, meetings cut from 60 to 30 to 15 minutes so the growth blocks never move. He is the opposite of the sleep-8-hours founder narrative and will say so.

7. Nobody is born a CEO. At 21 he paid his first advisor with free access to the software. The advice: "the CEO role is a lonely one, always." He has run advisors from day one, rotated them as he outgrew them, down to one person per channel. Openness as management: expectations set in the first interview, "run, fall, get up, but I always tell the truth."

8. Dyslexic CEO. Cannot sit and read a book. Everything he learns is audio: Jim Rohn and Tony Robbins instead of music in the morning, audiobooks, team summaries. Built a $20M+ ARR company without reading one business book cover to cover.

9. What comes after the exit. Stated dream: billionaire by 45, and not for the money. Money was for a skateboard and trips. The game is overcoming challenges, and after the exit he wanted to keep playing exactly the same game. AI take: go vertical, not horizontal. The companies that survive the next decade sit closest to one customer's specific pain.

## Why DOAC specifically

- Steven's audience wants childhood, money, mistakes and mindset with numbers attached. Every Lior story above has a number and a scene.
- He is a bootstrapped founder who sold to a public company, a rarer profile than the VC-backed unicorn founder DOAC usually books.
- He has already done long-form on record in English and Hebrew and is comfortable going deep on the failures, not only the wins.
- Angle for the pitch: the immigrant kid who never accepted a no, from a hacked family computer at 10 to a public-company exit at 28, without a single investor.

---

## Guardrails for the call, say it right

- Exit number: say "reported $92 million" or "a nine-figure deal was reported". Never state $92M as the official price, Fiverr never disclosed it.
- Company category: e-commerce automation, not "dropshipping software".
- Team size: 200 employees. Never "250" and never "100 engineers".
- He never got rejected by investors. He never approached any. The line is "didn't know raising was an option".
- Never say employees had or didn't have equity. Leave equity out.
- Shopify is a "partner". Never "affiliate".
- AutoDS operates independently inside Fiverr with Lior as CEO. Not "Fiverr's e-commerce unit".
- He does not read books. Never "he read" or "his favourite book". He listens.
- The PE walk-away was about protecting the team, never about holding out for a bigger number.
