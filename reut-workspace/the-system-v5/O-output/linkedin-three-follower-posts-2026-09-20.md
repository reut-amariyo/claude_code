## Post 1: the buyout he walked from

Killed a $30M buyout the day before signing.
(Fiverr closed in 23 days.)

Private equity, two years before the exit. Six months of negotiation. Full due diligence, every document, every call. Everyone ready to sign.

At that stage, $30M was life-changing money.

Back then walking away felt like the most expensive decision of my life. Today I think it was the cheapest.

The tell came late. In one of the final conversations the buyer laid out year one: cut the team, squeeze the margin, run it for cash.

I looked at the org chart and saw people who had been there since day one. Some of them since the apartment with the boxes.

Any experienced advisor would have said sign. Deals like that do not come back.

I did not sign.

Two years later a different buyer came with a different plan: keep the company, keep the people, keep building. That deal closed in 23 days.

The price is one line in the contract. The plan for your people is the rest of it.

If you ever sit at a table like that, ask one question early: what does this team look like twelve months after close. The answer tells you everything the term sheet does not.

## Post 2: Mom and the CTO

Mom asked if my CTO can still find a job.
(As a programmer.)

Saturday lunch, last week. Same table where, years ago, with thousands of paying customers already on AutoDS, she asked if I could still be a programmer.

She has never asked about revenue.
She never asked about the exit.
Every few years she asks whether the two of us will be OK.

200 people work at AutoDS now. Fiverr owns it. To my mom it is still two kids with a computer who might need a real job one day.

Watching her ask, I recognized something about running a company.

The people who love you never upgrade their fear. The company grows, the question stays the same size.

And the question is worth keeping. A founder who stops being asked "will you be OK" starts believing the answer is settled. It never is. Every quarter you earn it again.

Michael, if it ever comes to it, the reference letter is ready.

Mom, we are OK. Ask me again next year.

## Post 3: the acquisition scorecard (first-party artifact)

⚠️ Items 1 and 5 are on record (rival user groups / three-star reviews, team). Items 2, 3, 4 are
placeholders for the shape. The final post is built from Lior's voice note, questions below.

5 years inside my biggest rival's user group.
(Then we acquired them.)

DSM-Tool was the market leader when AutoDS started. Two founders, an MBA, connections, money. Everything I did not have at 21.

So I joined their user groups and read every complaint, for years.

That habit became the checklist we still run before buying a company. 4 acquisitions since, and the financials were never the first page.

The 5 things we score first:

1. The three-star reviews. Five stars tell you what they promise. One star tells you who is angry. Three stars tell you exactly what is missing, and whether we already have it.

2. Who the customers shout at. If support goes to the founder, the founder is the product. The day the founder leaves, the customers leave with him.

3. Where their users go the day after cancelling. If they come to us, we are buying speed. If they go elsewhere, we are buying a lesson.

4. The one feature their users keep asking for that we already shipped. That gap sets the price, their revenue does not.

5. Whether the team wants to stay. A product transfers in a week. A team that does not want to be there transfers nothing.

The financials come after all five. By then we already know if we want it. The numbers only decide the price.
